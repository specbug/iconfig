Body Text — Long-form reading

Typography is the craft of endowing human language with a durable visual form, and thus with an independent existence. Its heartbeat is a steady, even pulse — the rhythm of text on a page. When done well, the reader forgets about the type entirely and is absorbed in the content.

Good proportional sans-serif fonts walk a tightrope between neutrality and character. Too neutral, and the font is invisible in the worst way — indistinguishable from system defaults, generic and forgettable. Too characterful, and it becomes a distraction, calling attention to itself when it should be serving the words.

R Sans aims for the sweet spot: distinctive enough to feel intentional, quiet enough to disappear during sustained reading. The single-storey a, the tailed l, the spurred G — these are small signals that say "someone chose this carefully."

Numbers should feel at home in running text: the figures 0123456789 sit on the baseline with tabular or proportional spacing. Punctuation — commas, quotes, semicolons — should feel warm, not mechanical. The discretionary ligatures fi fl ff add a touch of refinement that most sans-serifs lack.

Lists

Design principles for R Sans:

  • Geometric warmth — Single-storey a, rounded quotes, softened terminals
  • Purposeful disambiguation — Tailed l, serifed I, slashed 0
  • Typographic refinement — Discretionary ligatures (fi, fl, ff)
  • Cohesive digit family — Flat-top 3, consistent contour language

Compared to stock Inter:

  1. Single-storey 'a' replaces the generic double-storey form
  2. Tailed 'l' prevents confusion with '1' and 'I'
  3. Serifed uppercase 'I' for instant recognition in body text
  4. Spurred 'G' adds structural clarity at small sizes
  5. Round quotes and commas for warmth over mechanical precision
  6. Discretionary ligatures for typographic sophistication

The Invisible Art of Type Selection

By Rishit V. — 7 Feb 2026 — 4 min read

A well-chosen typeface is like good posture: you only notice it when it's wrong. But the difference between "fine" and "right" compounds over hours of reading.

Most developers never think about their UI font. They inherit whatever the OS provides — San Francisco on macOS, Segoe UI on Windows, Roboto on Android — and move on. And honestly? Those defaults are excellent. They're the product of millions of dollars and years of research.

But defaults are designed for everyone, which means they're optimized for no one in particular. A custom font lets you make opinionated choices: do you prefer geometric warmth or humanist flexibility? Tabular or proportional figures? Ligatures in your UI copy?

The Single-Storey Question

The lowercase a is the most contentious letter in type design. The double-storey form (with its little hat) is traditional — inherited from Renaissance scribes. The single-storey form (a simple circle and stem) is modern — Futura made it mainstream in 1927.

For R Sans, single-storey wins. Here's why:

  • It signals intentionality — you know this isn't a default font
  • It pairs beautifully with R Mono's rounded a glyph
  • It reinforces the "circles meeting rectangles" geometric language

Compare: "application" with single-storey a vs. the stock double-storey. The geometric form creates a more rhythmic, open texture — especially in words with multiple a's.

Technical Details

R Sans is built from Inter 4.1 with character variants frozen via pyftfeatfreeze. The optical size axis is pinned to 16px for body text optimization, and the line gap is increased to 164 units for comfortable reading at 1.29× line-height.

pyftfeatfreeze -f "cv01,cv05,cv08,cv09,cv10,cv11,cv12,cv13,ss03,zero,dlig" \ -R 'Inter Variable/R Sans' \ InterVariable.ttf RSans-Variable.ttf

The build produces 16 static instances (8 weights × upright + italic) plus variable fonts for web use. Total web payload: ~350 KB for the variable font, or ~116 KB per static WOFF2.
